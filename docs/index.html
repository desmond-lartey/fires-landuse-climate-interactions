Published research · documentation site

Fire, land use, and climate interactions in West African conservation landscapes

A spatially explicit, buffer-based analysis of 20 years (2004–2024) of MODIS burned-area detections across 15 protected areas in Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Togo, Burkina Faso, Benin, and Nigeria — integrating harmonized GLAD/ESRI land cover and ERA5-Land climate reanalysis to explain why fire regimes differ across ecological zones, buffer distance, and vegetation type.

Published DOI 10.1177/19400829261486748 Journal Tropical Conservation Science Period 2004–2024 Protected areas 15 Ecological zones 4 License MIT (code)

About this study

Fire shapes vegetation structure, biodiversity, and carbon dynamics across West African protected areas (PAs), yet fine-scale interactions of climatic, ecological, and landscape controls on burning have remained poorly understood. This study analyzed fire dynamics across 15 PAs in four ecological zones, using 20 years (2004–2024) of MODIS burned area data, harmonized GLAD and ESRI land cover, and ERA5 climate reanalysis within a buffer-based spatial framework (0–20 km) that captures the gradient from PA interiors to surrounding human-modified landscapes.

Data inputs

Four remotely sensed layers

MODIS MCD64A1 burned area (500 m), harmonized GLAD 2000–2020 / ESRI 2021–2024 land cover (30 m / 10 m), and ERA5-Land monthly climate reanalysis (~25 km), reduced to park- and buffer-level zonal statistics in Google Earth Engine.

Spatial framework

Interior-to-edge gradients

Each park is analysed as five concentric units — the interior (0 km) and buffer rings at 5, 10, 15, and 20 km — so fire exposure, land cover, and climate can be compared from park core to surrounding landscape.

Headline findings

  • Northern Savanna parks burn hardest and most predictably. Mole, Comoé, and W (Benin) reach 57–61% of park area burned in December alone; Southern Forest parks (Bia, Marahoué) stay near zero regardless of season.
  • Dry-season rainfall is the dominant climatic control. Burned area rises as precipitation falls (pooled slope −371.08 km²/mm, p < 0.001); the effect is individually significant at Gashaka-Gumti (p = 0.014).
  • Fire exposure declines from park interior to edge — strongest in Northern Savanna, essentially flat in Southern Forest, where fire is ecologically constrained at any distance.
  • Ecological zone governs fire frequency more than land-cover class does. The same land-cover type burns at very different rates depending on which zone it sits in.
  • Fires outside park boundaries ignite earlier and burn more than fires inside, pointing to stronger anthropogenic ignition pressure in surrounding landscapes.

Full statistical detail is on the Findings page.
