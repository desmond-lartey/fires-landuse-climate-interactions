Results summary

What the twenty-year record shows

This page paraphrases the manuscript's five results sections and links each to its source figure. For exact wording, statistics, and figure images, see the manuscript itself, this site is a technical/methods companion, not a substitute for the paper.

Frequency of burning by protected area (Figure 3)

Burning concentrates in the early-to-mid dry season, October through January, but the extent differs sharply by park. Mole, Comoé, and W (Benin) show the highest burned-area proportions, with December averages reaching roughly 57–61% of park area. W (Burkina Faso), Gashaka-Gumti, and Kaboré Tambi show strong November–January peaks. All of these fall in the Northern Savanna zone. Bia, Marahoué, and Togo, by contrast, show near-zero burned area throughout the fire season. Grouped by zone, Northern Savanna forms a clear high-burning cluster, Northern Forest and Southern Transition are intermediate and variable, and Southern Forest is consistently near zero.

See the fire-season calendar on the homepage for the full 15-park, 6-month heatmap this section is drawn from.

Annual burning, rainfall, and temperature (Figures 4–6)

The rainfall–fire relationship is strongest in the dry season, when fuel moisture is lowest. Across the 2004–2024 record, fire-prone parks, Gashaka-Gumti, Mole, Comoé, Kainji Lake, and Kogyae, show negative slopes between precipitation and burned area, meaning burning increases as rainfall declines. Gashaka-Gumti is the only park with an individually statistically significant negative slope (−182.69 km²/mm, p = 0.014); others show the same direction without reaching significance, likely reflecting high interannual variability relative to record length. Parks with limited fire activity, Bia, Tiogo, Kyabobo, Marahoué, Bontioli, show flat or inconsistent slopes, indicating ecological rather than climatic constraints on burning. The pooled regression across all parks and years is significant (slope = −371.08 km²/mm, p < 0.001, R² = 0.04); explained variance is modest but the direction of the effect is consistent wherever fire is common.

The seasonal time series reinforces this: interannual peaks in dry-season burned area at fire-prone Northern Savanna parks align with drier years (notably around 2009, 2012, 2015–2016, and 2019). Wetter or forest-dominant parks show minimal dry-season fire regardless of rainfall variability. Wet-season burning is negligible almost everywhere, with occasional early-ignition exceptions at a handful of savanna parks.

Fire activity, buffer distance, and land cover (Figure 7)

Annual burned area declines with increasing distance from park boundaries, an effect strongest in the Northern Savanna zone, where fire frequency peaks within 5–10 km of the interior and then declines toward 20 km. The Southern Forest zone shows minimal change across buffers, fire is ecologically constrained regardless of distance. Parks and years with higher cumulative burned area (2004–2024) show larger tree-cover losses, mixed rangeland responses, moderate cropland gains at moderate fire exposure but declines at extreme exposure, and no clear built-up area relationship. Burned area itself varies by land-cover class within each ecological zone: in Northern Savanna, croplands, rangelands, and sparse vegetation all show high fire frequency; in Southern Forest, all classes including croplands stay low; Northern Forest and Southern Transition are intermediate, with grass- and crop-dominated areas burning more than tree cover.

Frequency, extent, and seasonal timing by vegetation type (Figure 8)

Burn frequency declines with distance from park boundaries across nearly all land-cover classes, with the sharpest reductions between 0 and 10 km, consistent with fuel structure, early-season burning, and human ignition concentrated near park edges. Rangelands show the highest proportional burned area, exceeding 60–80% of grass-dominated pixels within the 0 km buffer at Comoé, Mole, and W (Benin). Croplands show a similar pattern at Kainji Lake, Tiogo, and Kaboré Tambi, consistent with early dry-season agricultural burning. Tree-dominated areas stay low in forest-rich parks like Bia and Marahoué but burn more in open-woodland parks like W (Benin) and Gashaka-Gumti. Herbaceous wetlands burn extensively at W (Benin) and Gashaka-Gumti, reflecting seasonal grass dominance.

Fire timing inside vs. outside protected areas (Figure 9)

Fires outside park boundaries ignite earlier and burn more extensively than fires inside, particularly in savanna-dominated parks. At Mole, Kainji Lake, Old Oyo, Gashaka-Gumti, Kyabobo, and Togo, burned area outside the park rises sharply in November–December, peaking in December–January, while interior burning peaks one to two months later. This offset points to earlier ignition pressure in surrounding human-dominated landscapes, linked to agricultural burning and land clearing. Comoé and W (Benin) show synchronized inside/outside timing but higher magnitude outside. Forest-dominated or moist-transition parks (Bia, Bontioli) show little distinction between inside and outside, fire spread is ecologically constrained regardless of boundary position.
